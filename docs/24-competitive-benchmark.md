# Competitive benchmark — the wardrobe-app category

*Researched 12–13 August 2026. Every figure below is sourced; where a number is
self-reported by the company or comes from a rival's marketing page, it says so.
Where sources disagree, both numbers are printed rather than averaged.*

The category has consolidated around three serious players — **Whering** (UK),
**Acloset** (Korea), and **Alta** (US) — plus a long tail of older apps that
survive on one-time fees, ads, or item caps. This document benchmarks all of
them against Toile, and is honest about where Toile loses.

### The five shapes of a wardrobe-app paywall

1. **The item cap** — Acloset and GetWardrobe both stop at exactly 100 items. A
   real wardrobe is 150–300 pieces, so the cap is calibrated to bite right after
   you have done the photographing.
2. **The analytics wall** — Indyx and Cladwell give away the cataloguing and
   charge for cost-per-wear and utilization. You do eight hours of labour; they
   sell you the conclusion. An App Store reviewer of Indyx puts it exactly:
   *"quality of life features are behind a paywall that don't need to be."*
3. **The sync wall** — Smart Closet charges $9.99/yr for backup, i.e. for the
   right not to lose your work.
4. **Ads** — Pureple, Combyne.
5. **Commerce** — the one that looks free. Whering and Alta charge you nothing
   and earn when you buy. Whering's lead investor *is* eBay; Alta has 4,000
   brand partners and an investor memo that names affiliate marketing as the
   market.

**The honest conclusion: "free" in this category almost always means
commerce-funded.** Only two models in the whole field are not paid for by your
future purchases — Stylebook's $4.99 one-time fee, and Toile, which has no
revenue at all because it has no server to run.

## 4. Trust — what they collect, per their own disclosures

From each app's **Google Play Data Safety** declaration, which is a legal
statement by the developer, not a review.

| | Collects | **Shares with third parties** | Advertising use |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Whering** | Name, email, phone, **photos and videos**, contacts, in-app messages, **precise location**, device IDs, app interactions | **Photos and videos** | Yes — app interactions used for advertising/marketing |
| **Acloset** | Name, email, user IDs, photos, **purchase history**, approximate **and precise location**, device IDs | Approximate location; **precise location — for advertising or marketing** | Yes |
| **Alta** | Name, email, phone, user IDs, **in-app search history**, photos, approximate location | Email, user IDs, phone, device IDs, search history — for analytics | Not declared |
| **Stylebook** | On device and iCloud only. *"We can't see or access your closet data."* | None | None |
| **Toile** | Nothing. There is no server, no account, no analytics, no network call | Nothing | None |

Whering sharing **photos and videos** with third parties is the single most
striking line in the category: users photograph every garment they own, and that
disclosure covers those photographs. Acloset sharing **precise location for
advertising** is the second.

## 5. What reviewers actually complain about

Aggregated from App Store and Google Play reviews:

- **The setup wall.** Universally named as the reason people quit: photographing
  and tagging a 100-item wardrobe takes 6–8 hours in Stylebook, and hours in the
  rest. *"The single biggest reason people download a closet app and never
  actually use it."*
- **Whering:** frequent crashes, lag, upload button failures, background removal
  "correct about 50% of the time", deleted items reappearing or vanishing, and a
  request for one-time pricing over subscriptions.
- **Acloset:** outfit suggestions feel "wonky", items that won't save, no
  occasion-based recommendations, and the app struggling at very large closet
  sizes.
- **Alta:** almost none — 4.9★ is the highest in the field. The recurring note is
  suspicion rather than fault: *"when does cost come into play?"*
- **Indyx:** the analytics paywall, and drag-and-drop slowing down past ~250 items.
- **The category:** fashion apps average roughly **28% retention at 90 days**,
  i.e. about seven in ten people are gone within three months.

## 6. Where Toile stands

### Where Toile genuinely wins

1. **The ledger is free.** Cost-per-wear, utilization, monthly activity, the
   brand table and the resting-money line are the *paid* tier at Indyx ($74.99/yr)
   and Cladwell ($95.88/yr). Toile gives away the exact artefact two competitors
   have built a subscription business on.
2. **No cap, ever.** Acloset and GetWardrobe both stop at 100 items. Toile has no
   number in it.
3. **Nothing to sell you.** Every free competitor is either commerce-funded
   (Whering/eBay, Alta/4,000 brands, Combyne/50 retailers) or ad-funded. Toile's
   *Before you buy* screen shows what you already own and then stops talking —
   a feature that is structurally impossible for a company earning affiliate fees.
4. **No account, no server, no photograph leaving the device.** Only Stylebook
   matches this, and only on iOS for a fee.
5. **Features nobody else has:** plans that don't inflate wear counts, a silent
   cooling-off wishlist, mending as a first-class state, retire-with-history, and
   copy law that forbids shame mechanics, streaks, badges and notifications.
6. **The setup wall answered without a server.** The VLM intake prompt hands the
   photograph to whatever model the user already pays for, and takes back a file.
   Alta solves the same problem by processing your photos on its infrastructure.

### Where Toile genuinely loses

1. **Distribution.** Whering has 10M users, Acloset 7M, Alta a TIME award. Toile
   has a GitHub Pages URL. This is the whole gap.
2. **No native app.** Every serious competitor is in both stores; Toile is a web
   page. No store listing, no store search, no install prompt, no push (though
   push is a deliberate veto).
3. **No automatic background removal.** Whering, Acloset, Alta and Indyx all cut
   garments out automatically; Alta uses Meta's Segment Anything. Toile asks for
   a photo or falls back to a drawn flat. This is the most visible daily quality
   gap.
4. **No cross-device sync.** Local-first means the record lives on one browser
   on one device. JSON export is a backup, not sync. Competitors all sync.
5. **No AI stylist, no weather, no try-on avatar.** These are the three features
   the market is currently competing on, and Toile has none of them.
6. **No social layer.** Whering, Acloset, Alta and Indyx all run communities;
   community is a retention mechanism Toile has declined.

## 7. What this argues for building next

**Build, in order:**

1. **Installability (PWA).** A manifest, an icon, an offline service worker and
   an "Add to Home Screen" prompt closes most of the native-app gap without a
   store, a company, or a server. Highest leverage item on this list.
2. **On-device background removal.** A WASM segmentation model running in the
   browser would match the category's headline feature *without* uploading a
   photograph — which is a stronger position than any competitor holds, not just
   parity. This is the one feature worth real engineering.
3. **Sync you own.** Not a server — an export/import round-trip over a file the
   user controls (a QR handoff, or a file in their own cloud drive). Answers the
   single-device objection without becoming a custodian.
4. **Weather, computed locally.** A latitude-free, coarse forecast is enough for
   "it will be cold"; it does not require identifying the user.

**Do not build:** resale links, affiliate shopping, a social feed, streaks,
notifications, or an item cap. Each is somebody else's revenue model, and three
of them are already vetoed by the focus group.

**The positioning that the research supports:** the category's free apps are free
because they earn when you buy, and the honest ones charge you for your own
numbers. Toile is the third option.

## 8. A correction owed to our own README

`README.md` currently states: *"~78% of users abandon closet apps within six
weeks."* **This figure could not be substantiated.** No source for it exists in
public research. The defensible replacement, which is nearly as strong:
**fashion apps average about 28% retention at 90 days.** The README should be
corrected.

Also note that the market-size figures circulating for this category are
worthless: SEO "market report" vendors variously price it at $144M, $2.7B and
$3.5B for the same year. None are cited here as fact.
